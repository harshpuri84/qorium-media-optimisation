Picking the next four cell-culture recipes

A primer for someone who knows logistics and data, but not biotech. Read this before the papers. The finished analysis, memo and code are in the public repo.

The brief, simply

A lab grows living cells in a liquid food called culture media. Media is a recipe. Change the recipe and the cells grow better or worse. Some ingredients are expensive.

Each recipe test takes days, costs money, and gives a noisy answer. The lab can test only 3 to 5 new recipes in the next batch.

You get the recipes that were already tested and their results. Your job is to choose the next 3 to 5 recipes and explain why. The choice must balance four things:

  • Performance: recipes the model expects to do well.
  • Cost: cheaper recipes are worth more at production scale.
  • Uncertainty: some recipes are worth testing only because you know little about that area.
  • Feasibility: the lab must be able to mix it (fractions add to 100%, pipettes have a minimum step).

The panel does not want the best model. They want a sensible bet under limited, noisy data, and a clear reason for each of the four picks.

Who Qorium is

Qorium is a Maastricht biotech company. It takes a small sample of skin cells from a living cow, grows them in the lab, and turns them into collagen-based leather. It raised €22 million in November 2025 and is installing new bioreactors (large stainless tanks where cells grow) to scale up.

Why this case matters to them: at production scale, media is one of the largest running costs. A recipe that keeps the cells healthy at lower cost per litre changes the unit economics. Their real outcome is probably cell growth and collagen output, not the PBMC viability in our dataset. Say this openly in the memo.

Sources: Pulse 2.0 on the €22M round, Silicon Canals, Business Wire, Oct 2024.

Biology you need (and no more)

Cells and media

  • Cell culture: keeping cells alive and growing outside the body, in plastic plates or tanks, at body temperature.
  • Basal medium: the base liquid with sugars, salts, amino acids and vitamins. DMEM and RPMI 1640 are two classic ones, sold by many suppliers.
  • FBS (fetal bovine serum): a blood-derived additive that helps cells grow. It is expensive, varies batch to batch, and is a problem for animal-free products. Cutting it is a major goal in cultivated meat and leather.
  • Serum-free medium: a ready-made commercial medium that does not need FBS. X-VIVO 15 (Lonza) and AR5 (CellGenix) are two. They cost more per litre than basal media.

Outcomes

  • Viability: percentage of cells alive. A cell can be alive and still not grow.
  • Proliferation: how much the cell count increases. This is closer to what a production company cares about.
  • AOPI staining: two dyes that mark live cells green and dead cells red. A machine then counts each colour. That is how our viability numbers were made.

The cells in our dataset

PBMCs (peripheral blood mononuclear cells) are white blood cells taken from human blood. They are used in drug testing and cell therapy. They are hard to keep alive outside the body: standard media lose viability and change which cell types survive. They have nothing to do with leather. They are only a stand-in dataset, chosen because it is small, public, and has a clean recipe structure.

How Bayesian optimisation picks experiments

The idea in four steps:

  1. Fit a model to the results so far. A Gaussian process (GP) gives two things for any untested recipe: a best guess, and how unsure it is.
  2. Score every possible recipe with an acquisition function. UCB is the simplest: score = guess + κ × uncertainty. Large κ favours exploring; small κ favours exploiting.
  3. Run the highest-scoring recipe in the lab.
  4. Add the result, refit, repeat.

The toy below uses one ingredient on a 0 to 100% axis, so you can see it. The real problem has three free dimensions (four fractions that add to 100%), but the logic is the same.

Three ideas to notice in the demo

  • The band shrinks where you have tested. Away from data the model admits it does not know.
  • κ = 0 gets stuck. It keeps testing next to the current best and can miss a better peak. Raise κ and it explores.
  • Noise matters. Each result in the demo has random noise added, as in a real assay. The curve does not pass through every point.

Picking a batch, not one at a time

The lab runs 4 recipes in parallel, so you cannot wait for each result. Constant liar (or "kriging believer") solves this. Pick the best recipe, pretend its result is already known (use the model's guess), refit, and pick again. The pretend result shrinks uncertainty there, so the next pick moves elsewhere. Repeat four times.

Adding cost

Two common ways. Constraint: only allow recipes below a cost ceiling, then maximise viability. Desirability: combine viability and cost into one score, as Cosenza 2022 did. The first is easier to explain to a lab manager.

The six "what ifs" the brief asks about

The final versions are in the memo, section "How the strategy changes". The short form is below.

If…Then the strategy changes like this
Only 10 to 20 prior experimentsUse a simpler model (a few parameters), favour exploring, and spend part of the batch on space-filling picks. Do not trust tight uncertainty bands.
Cheap assays are noisyUse them to screen many recipes. Model noise explicitly and repeat readings on promising ones.
Expensive assays are reliableSpend them only on finalists. A multi-fidelity model learns how the cheap assay maps to the expensive one (Cosenza's approach).
Cost data is incompleteUse catalogue prices, labelled as estimates, plus a sensitivity sweep. Show which picks change if a price doubles.
Some components are categorical or constrainedHandle categories (e.g. which sugar to use) with a separate kernel or one model per category. Encode limits as hard rules in the candidate generator. The K. phaffii data in the same paper has a categorical carbon source.
Experiments run in parallel batchesPick the batch jointly (constant liar, or q-acquisition functions). Force diversity so four picks are not near-copies.

Why a GP if it fails the forward test? Because it gives a usable rule for choosing a diverse batch that weighs "likely good" against "we know little here". It is not used as a forecast. The decision rests on comparing each new blend with E19 on the same plate.

The recommendation

Five formulations, the most the brief allows. Four wells each, 20 wells in total, in random positions on the inner part of a 96-well plate.

Decision rule, set before the plate runs. A blend passes the screen if its mean is no more than 5 points below the same-plate E19, at equal or lower cost. Four wells cannot prove two blends are equal: the uncertainty on the difference is about ±11 points, so the screen only filters. First the assay owner checks the plate is valid; an invalid plate is repeated. One finalist then goes to a paired confirmation against E19 across independent cell and medium preparations, sized from the variance this pilot measures. It passes if the one-sided lower bound on the difference stays above −5 points. For scale: 20 wells per arm would give only about 51% power, and about 45 would give 80%.

The Qorium catch. These media carry FBS (serum from calves). E19 is 6.5% FBS by volume, and every pick raises that; the "cheaper" pick reaches 9.9%. Qorium says it uses no animal products beyond the cells. So the recipes do not transfer. A real Qorium campaign starts from an animal-product-free ingredient list. Only the method transfers.

In logistics terms: this plate is a pilot on four lanes plus a control lane. A pilot that looks good gets a proper trial before anyone changes the network.

Notes to own in the interview

Say these before the panel does. Each one is a weakness that turns into evidence of judgement once you name it.

"The model fails the forward test."

Yes. Every model missed round 3 by 22 to 29 points, and the GP's forward ranking was weak. That is why I use it only to propose a diverse batch, and why E19 is on the plate. The decision is a same-plate comparison, not a forecast.

"The model only really sees two ingredients."

Its RPMI-10 and AR5 settings sit at the upper limit, so it treats them as interchangeable. All four picks hold less AR5 than E19, so the plate tests that assumption directly. Limiting the model moved the picks by 2% at most.

"Your prices are guesses."

Partly. Basal media and X-VIVO 15 come from supplier pages. FBS is a search snippet, and FBS is 74 to 80% of the supplemented cost. AR5 has no public price. So cost is a constraint checked across 9 scenarios, and real quotes are the first thing I would buy.

"Why not just pick the top four predictions?"

In simulation, always picking the current best was no better than random, and worse on one truth. Mixing roles beat random in 19 to 23 of 30 seeds. That supports mixing roles. It does not prove one exploration slot is the right split.

"What does this mean for Qorium?"

PBMC viability is a stand-in. The same loop would run on bovine skin-cell growth and collagen output, with a cheap screening assay paired with an expensive confirmatory one, and real supplier quotes. The schema already supports that without changes.

"How did you work?" (suggested answer, adjust to your own view)

I used AI agents for the analysis and code, and ran three independent model reviews against my own work. Each review found real problems, and each fix is logged with the before and after values. That is how I would run a data science team: fast drafts, adversarial review, every change traceable.

Check yourself

Why can you not treat the four fractions as four independent inputs?

They add to 100%. Fix three and the fourth is set. There are only three free dimensions, and changing one fraction always changes another.

Round 3 scored much higher. Does that prove the optimiser worked?

Not alone. Round 3 recipes are all similar, and they were run at a different time. A better batch of cells or a better day in the lab would look the same. Re-running E19 next to new recipes would help separate the two.

E14 scored 7.52%, next to a recipe that scored 48.15%. What do you do with it?

Keep it, and show results with and without it. Its readings agree with each other, so the whole run probably failed or the biology has a sharp drop. The data cannot tell which.

Why would you pick a recipe the model expects to be mediocre?

Because the model is very unsure there. One test can remove a large blind spot, which improves every later decision. That is the explore part of the batch.

How does viability relate to Qorium's business?

Loosely. Qorium needs skin cells to grow and make collagen at low cost per litre. Viability is a necessary first step, not the goal. The method transfers. The numbers do not.
